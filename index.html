Machine-learning interatomic potentials give computational scientists a fast way to calculate the energies, forces and stresses of atomic systems — but no single model is best everywhere. OxideFlow runs oxide surface chemistry on these potentials end to end, with no DFT in the loop, and benchmarks each model against published results so the right one is chosen for each surface and adsorbate chemistry.

A pipeline that searches instead of guesses

OxideFlow takes a bulk crystal and turns it into a searched minimum surface energy, running every calculation on a machine-learning potential. It samples different vacancies or adsorption configurations, relaxes every candidate and keeps the lowest one.

Seconds, not hours

Placing a molecule on a surface and letting it settle took the models 22 to 158 seconds each. The quantum-chemistry equivalent takes hours. Measured on 15 machine-learning potentials, averaged over O and OH adsorption on rutile oxides.

Right order, wrong number

Scored against the largest reference set, Comer et al.'s DFT study of O and OH on 33 rutile oxides (J. Phys. Chem. C 2022), the best models put materials in nearly the same order as quantum chemistry, while missing the actual energy by 0.3 to 0.9 eV, enough to change a conclusion.

Why it matters. Decide what the potential is for before you use it. For ranking surfaces, the best models are close to DFT. For predicting a final energy, they are off by tenths of an electron-volt, and the model that ranks best is not the one that predicts best.

The chemistry decides

7 of 33 oxides in the Comer et al. set converge too unreliably to score, because the rutile structure they were forced into is unstable for them. The magnetic row converges but carries the largest errors. Two models trained only on metals predict that pulling oxygen out of titanium dioxide releases energy instead of taking it.

Which model, for which chemistry

Put the findings together and the benchmark becomes a lookup: for each surface and adsorbate, the model that reproduces the reference best. That lookup is what OxideFlow carries alongside the pipeline, so the speed is used with a model that has earned trust on that chemistry.

Conclusion

OxideFlow offers a new way to evaluate MLIPs: not on structures DFT has already relaxed, but through a full workflow. Tested like this, each surface chemistry and adsorbate can be matched to the model that handles it best, giving more accurate energies and trend predictions and moving MLIPs a step closer to running surface chemistry fully independent of DFT.

Roadmap

OxideFlow was originally built for propane oxidative dehydrogenation (ODH) on perovskite catalysts; the rutile benchmark was the proving ground. The pipeline is now growing back toward that reaction.

  • Beyond rutile. A surface builder for other crystal families, starting with the perovskite catalysts the project began on.
  • Bond breaking. The C–H activation energy that decides whether propane converts to propene.
  • Larger molecules. Orientation sampling for adsorbates with more than one way to land.
How the benchmark was run

Fifteen checkpoints across six model families (UMA, MACE-MH-1, SevenNet-omni, eSEN, Orb-v2, CHGNet), chosen from public leaderboards; multi-task models were run once per DFT flavour they imitate. Each was run through OxideFlow and scored against published DFT, but the reference sets are very different in size. The O and OH benchmarks use Comer et al.'s study of all 33 rutile MO2 oxides on two facets, so they carry most of the weight on this page. The remaining tests (CO, H, water and oxygen-vacancy formation) rest on one to three published values each, from four other studies, and are reported as single comparisons rather than trends. Settings match the reference studies where known; every result is a static, zero-temperature relaxation. All runs on UBC's ARC Sockeye cluster.
